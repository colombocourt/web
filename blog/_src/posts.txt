<p>Guests ask us this before they ask anything else, and the answer they usually get is a laminated card. So here is the version we would give a friend, written by the people who actually eat here.</p>

<p>Colombo 3 is Kollupitiya, the strip between Galle Road and the sea, and it is the part of the city where you can still eat well on foot. That matters more than it sounds. Colombo traffic does not move in the evening, and a restaurant twenty minutes away by map can be fifty minutes away at seven o&rsquo;clock.</p>

<h2>Within walking distance</h2>

<p>Arcade Independence Square is the easiest evening in the neighbourhood: a restored colonial building with a dozen places to eat in it, which means a table for a group who cannot agree. It is about fifteen minutes on foot from Alfred House Avenue and it is lit well enough to walk back from.</p>

<p>Galle Face Green is the other one, and it is not really a restaurant at all. It is a long stretch of grass beside the sea where the isso wade and the kottu carts set up in the late afternoon. Go at about half past five, eat standing up, and watch the whole city do the same thing.</p>

<h2>Worth a tuk-tuk</h2>

<p>Colombo City Centre has a cinema and a food court above it, which is the correct answer on a wet evening with children. The Dutch Hospital in Fort is the handsome one: a seventeenth-century courtyard with restaurants down both sides, ten minutes by tuk-tuk and worth doing once for the building alone.</p>

<p>Pettah is a daytime trip rather than an evening one. Go for the market and the street food, not for dinner, and go early.</p>

<h2>The thing nobody tells you</h2>

<p>Sunday night in Colombo is quieter than you expect, and Poya days, the monthly full moon holidays, are quieter still. Alcohol is not served in most places on Poya, and some kitchens close entirely. Ask us which one is coming and we will tell you straight, because arriving somewhere shut is a bad end to a good day.</p>

<p>Book by half past six for a Friday or Saturday anywhere good. Most places take a WhatsApp message and most of them answer it.</p>

<figure><img src="{{A}}assets/img/bl-lights-700.jpg" srcset="{{A}}assets/img/bl-lights-700.jpg 700w, {{A}}assets/img/bl-lights-1100.jpg 1100w" sizes="(max-width: 760px) 92vw, 46rem" width="700" height="467" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="Brass and glass pendant lights hanging in a dark interior at Colombo Court."></figure>

<h2>And here</h2>

<p>We have two, and we would say so even if we did not own them. <a href="/eat-drink/#amber">Amber Poolside</a> is the daytime one, at the water&rsquo;s edge under the trees, from breakfast at seven through lunch and high tea. <a href="/eat-drink/#cloud">Cloud Caf&eacute;</a> is the evening one, a rooftop with a glass roof over part of it, open to everyone until half past eleven.</p>

<p>Both take walk-ins. Neither requires you to be staying here.</p>

<p>Bandaranaike International, which everyone calls Katunayake and the airlines call CMB, is about <strong>35 km</strong> north of the city. Allow <strong>45 to 60 minutes</strong> to Colombo 3. <h2>The four ways</h2>

<p><strong>A hotel transfer.</strong> The simplest, and the only one where somebody is standing in arrivals with your name. We arrange it on request: send us the flight number when you book and it is done. You pay the hotel, not the driver, and the price does not change because it is raining.</p>

<p><strong>An airport taxi.</strong> There is a counter in the arrivals hall with fixed prices to each part of the city. Reliable, metered, and you will not be negotiating at two in the morning after eleven hours in the air.</p>

<p><strong>A ride app.</strong> PickMe is the local one and Uber also runs here. Both are cheaper than a taxi and both require a working phone, which is the catch on arrival. There is a designated pick-up area rather than a kerbside free-for-all.</p>

<p><strong>The expressway bus.</strong> The cheapest by a wide margin and genuinely fine: an air-conditioned coach on the E03 to Makumbura, then a short ride into town. It is the right answer if you are travelling light and the wrong one with three suitcases.</p>

<p>High tea in Sri Lanka is not the English institution wearing a hat. It grew out of the same colonial habit, then spent a century in a country that grows the tea, and it came out the other side as something else: a proper afternoon meal, more savoury than sweet, eaten later than an Englishman would.</p>

<h2>What goes on the stand</h2>

<p>Two versions, and the choice matters more than people expect.</p>

<p>The <strong>Ceylon</strong> stand is the local one. Cutlets and patties, devilled things, spring rolls, a small fish bun, sambol on the side, and something jaggery-sweet at the top. It is spiced, it is filling, and it is what a Sri Lankan family orders.</p>

<p>The <strong>English</strong> stand is the one you are picturing. Finger sandwiches with the crusts off, scones with cream, small cakes and tarts above them. Gentler, and the right answer if you have been eating curry for four days.</p>

<figure><img src="{{A}}assets/img/bl-ceylontea-700.jpg" srcset="{{A}}assets/img/bl-ceylontea-700.jpg 700w, {{A}}assets/img/bl-ceylontea-1100.jpg 1100w" sizes="(max-width: 760px) 92vw, 46rem" width="700" height="467" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="A Ceylon high tea stand beside the pool, cutlets, spring rolls and small savouries on three tiers with cups of tea alongside."><figcaption>The Ceylon stand, which is the one to order if you have never had it.</figcaption></figure>

<h2>The tea itself</h2>

<p>Ask where it is from. Sri Lankan tea is not one thing: high-grown from Nuwara Eliya is light and briskly astringent, Uva has a sharper edge, and low-grown Ruhuna is dark and malty and takes milk without complaint. If you have been drinking supermarket tea labelled Ceylon, it was probably a blend of all three.</p>

<p>And then there is the honest confession of the modern Colombo afternoon: a great many people order iced coffee instead, and nobody minds. It is thirty degrees outside.</p>

<h2>When to go</h2>

<p>The best ninety minutes are between four and half past five. The light has come off the top of the heat, the pool is still warm enough to get into afterwards, and the city has not yet started going home. Before four it is simply hot. After six you should be having a drink instead.</p>

<h2>Here</h2>

<p>High tea runs from <strong>15:30 to 18:30</strong> every day, at <strong>LKR 2,800 net per person</strong>, and the price includes a pot of tea, a coffee or an iced coffee. You can have it beside the water at Amber Poolside or up on the roof at Cloud Caf&eacute; as the light goes, and it is open to everyone, not only to people staying here.</p>

<p>A message the day before is enough. <a href="/eat-drink/#whats-on">The offers page has the detail</a>, or send us a note on WhatsApp.</p>

=== what-carbon-neutral-actually-means
title: What a carbon neutral hotel actually does
date: 2026-09-02
topic: Sustainability
summary: We have been certified CarbonNeutral since 2011, which was the first for a city hotel in South Asia. Here is what that involves in a building, rather than in a brochure.
hero: bl-carbon-hero
heroW: 1600
heroAlt: A tree sculpted from reclaimed steel and rust rising through a glass roof at Colombo Court.

<p>Carbon neutral is a claim that has been used badly enough to be worth explaining. Here is what it means in this particular building, including the parts that are less flattering.</p>

<h2>It started with not knocking anything down</h2>

<p>Colombo Court was not built as a hotel. It was two office buildings, and the single largest thing we ever did for our footprint was to keep them. The embodied carbon in a concrete frame is enormous, and demolishing one to put up something greener is usually a net loss for decades. So the frames stayed, the court was cut into the middle of them, and the rooms were fitted into what was already there. That is also why no two rooms are quite the same shape.</p>

<h2>What is in the building</h2>

<p>Solar panels heat the water. The lighting is LED throughout, which sounds trivial until you count the fittings in thirty-one rooms and four public spaces. Rainwater is harvested off the roofs, and the water from the plunge pool and the laundry is recycled into the garden rather than sent to the drain, which is why the court is as green as it is in February.</p>

<p>Drinking water is filtered and bottled in glass on site. That one is worth naming because of the number it removes: a hotel this size can get through tens of thousands of plastic bottles a year, and we keep ours to the occasional week when the bottling cannot keep up.</p>

<figure><img src="{{A}}assets/img/bl-tree-700.jpg" srcset="{{A}}assets/img/bl-tree-700.jpg 700w, {{A}}assets/img/bl-tree-1100.jpg 1100w" sizes="(max-width: 760px) 92vw, 46rem" width="700" height="467" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="A sculpture of a tree welded from scrap steel, rising through the building towards a glass roof."><figcaption>The tree in the stairwell is welded from scrap. So is a good deal of the art.</figcaption></figure>

<h2>The furniture and the art</h2>

<p>Timber here is reclaimed rather than felled, the paper is recycled, and much of the art is made from scrap metal by Sri Lankan artists, including the tree above. None of this is decoration with a story attached afterwards. It is what you do when the brief is to build a hotel without buying very much.</p>

<h2>The part that is offsetting</h2>

<p>And here is the honest bit. No hotel runs at zero. Air conditioning in Colombo is not optional, guests arrive on aeroplanes, and a kitchen burns gas. What certification means is that the remaining emissions are measured every year by an independent assessor and then offset, and that the measuring is the hard part rather than the offsetting. We have done it every year since 2011.</p>

<p>Offsetting is not the same as not emitting, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. What it does mean is that the number is counted rather than guessed, and that it goes down most years.</p>

<figure><img src="{{A}}assets/img/bl-court-700.jpg" srcset="{{A}}assets/img/bl-court-700.jpg 700w, {{A}}assets/img/bl-court-1100.jpg 1100w" sizes="(max-width: 760px) 92vw, 46rem" width="700" height="467" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="The planted courtyard at Colombo Court, tables under dense greenery beside the lap pool."></figure>

<h2>What it asks of you</h2>

<p>Very little, is the short answer. There is a card in the bathroom about towels, and a towel left on the floor is changed that day, but the larger choices were made in the building rather than handed to the guest, which we think is the right way round.</p>

<p>Colombo is treated as an airport with a city attached. Two days will not fix that reputation but they will show you where it came from, and the trick is not to try to see everything. Here is the order we would do it in.</p>

<h2>Day one, morning</h2>

<p>Start with the Gangaramaya Temple, and start early: it opens at first light and it is a different place at seven in the morning than at eleven. Cover your shoulders and knees, take your shoes off, and give it an hour rather than twenty minutes. Beira Lake is next to it and the Seema Malaka temple sits out on the water, which is the photograph everyone takes and is worth taking.</p>

<p>Then Fort, on foot. The Dutch Hospital is the handsome bit, a seventeenth-century courtyard with shops and restaurants down both sides. The old lighthouse clock tower is five minutes away.</p>

<h2>Day one, afternoon</h2>

<p>Pettah, if you have the energy for it. It is the wholesale market district and it is loud, crowded and completely unpolished, which is precisely the point. The Red Mosque is the landmark. Go before three, drink water, and accept that you will not find whatever you came for.</p>

<p>Then come back, get in the pool for an hour, and change.</p>

<figure><img src="{{A}}assets/img/bl-court-700.jpg" srcset="{{A}}assets/img/bl-court-700.jpg 700w, {{A}}assets/img/bl-court-1100.jpg 1100w" sizes="(max-width: 760px) 92vw, 46rem" width="700" height="467" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="The planted courtyard at Colombo Court, tables under greenery beside the lap pool."><figcaption>The middle of the afternoon is for this, not for sightseeing.</figcaption></figure>

<h2>Day one, evening</h2>

<p>Galle Face Green at half past five. A kilometre of grass along the sea, the entire city out walking on it, and food carts the whole length. Eat isso wade standing up. Stay for the sunset, which happens quickly and directly into the Indian Ocean.</p>

<h2>Day two</h2>

<p>Independence Square in the morning, which is a monument surrounded by the nicest walking in the city, and the Arcade next to it for coffee and air conditioning. The National Museum is a short ride away and is better than it looks from outside: the Kandyan regalia alone is worth the entrance.</p>

<p>Give the afternoon to something slower. An hour of Balinese massage, a swim, a late lunch that turns into the rest of the day. Colombo in the middle of the afternoon is a city to be indoors in, and fighting that is how people end up disliking it.</p>

<p>Finish on a rooftop with the city lit underneath you. There are several and ours is one of them.</p>

<figure><img src="{{A}}assets/img/bl-lights-700.jpg" srcset="{{A}}assets/img/bl-lights-700.jpg 700w, {{A}}assets/img/bl-lights-1100.jpg 1100w" sizes="(max-width: 760px) 92vw, 46rem" width="700" height="467" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="Brass and glass pendant lights hanging against a dark interior at Colombo Court."></figure>

<h2>Practical notes</h2>

<p>Tuk-tuks are everywhere; use a meter or a ride app rather than agreeing a price. Sunday is quiet and Poya days, the monthly full moon holidays, are quieter still, with no alcohol served. Dress modestly for temples and carry a bottle of water everywhere. And do not book anything for the hour after two in the afternoon, because you will not want to go.</p>

<p>Two names appear on almost every spa list in Sri Lanka, and most people pick whichever word they recognise. They are genuinely different hours, so it is worth knowing which one you are booking.</p>

<h2>Balinese</h2>

<p>Balinese massage is firm, rhythmic and it works on muscle. Palms, thumbs, knuckles and forearms, long strokes down the length of a limb, and enough pressure to find the thing in your shoulder that you have been carrying since the flight. Warm oil throughout, and stretching worked in towards the end.</p>

<p>Book it if you have been sitting still for eleven hours, if you carry tension in your neck and shoulders, or if you want to feel the effect immediately. It is the one to have on the day you arrive.</p>

<h2>Ayurvedic</h2>

<p>Ayurvedic treatment comes out of a much older system, and the massage is one part of it rather than the whole. The pressure is gentler and the strokes follow the lines the tradition holds the body&rsquo;s energy runs along. The oils are the point: they are chosen for you, warmed, and used generously, and they are usually herbal preparations pressed here in Sri Lanka rather than a scented base oil.</p>

<p>Book it if you want the slower thing. It is less of a fix and more of a reset, and the effect tends to arrive that evening rather than as you sit up.</p>

<figure><img src="{{A}}assets/img/bl-spa-700.jpg" srcset="{{A}}assets/img/bl-spa-700.jpg 700w, {{A}}assets/img/bl-spa-1080.jpg 1080w" sizes="(max-width: 760px) 92vw, 46rem" width="700" height="467" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="Oils, salt and rolled towels set out for a treatment at The Court Spa."><figcaption>The oils are chosen after the conversation, not before it.</figcaption></figure>

<h2>How to choose</h2>

<p>The short version. Sore, stiff, just landed, want it fixed: Balinese. Frazzled, not sleeping, want to be put back together rather than worked on: Ayurvedic. Never had either, and would rather feel something happen: Balinese, and firm.</p>

<p>Sixty minutes is enough for a first treatment. Ninety is better for Ayurvedic, because the slow part is where the value is.</p>

<h2>What actually happens</h2>

<p>You are met by the therapist rather than a form. She will ask what you do, what hurts, whether anything is injured and how much pressure you want, and she will ask it before you are on the bed rather than through a towel. A foot ritual opens every treatment and it is inside the hour rather than added to it.</p>

<p>Then the treatment. A robe and slippers are in the room, there is a locker for everything else, and you are covered throughout.</p>

=== colombo-when-it-rains

<p>People cancel Sri Lankan trips over the word monsoon, which is a shame, because the thing the word describes is not what they are imagining. Colombo does not get grey days. It gets an hour.</p>

<h2>The two monsoons</h2>

<p>The south-west monsoon runs roughly <strong>May to September</strong> and is the one that affects Colombo and the west coast. The north-east monsoon, roughly <strong>October to January</strong>, mostly affects the other side of the island, though the inter-monsoon weeks in October and November bring thunderstorms here too.</p>

<p>So the wettest months in Colombo are May, June, October and November. And what happens in them is usually this: a bright morning, a build-up after lunch, forty minutes of rain heavy enough to be genuinely impressive, and then an evening that smells like wet leaves and is five degrees cooler than the day before.</p>

<h2>What to do with the hour</h2>

<p>Do not fight it. Nobody in Colombo is out in it either, which is why the rain is also the best hour of the day to be somewhere good indoors.</p>

<p>The National Museum is the obvious one and it is genuinely good. Arcade Independence Square is a restored colonial building with shops, coffee and air conditioning under one roof. Colombo City Centre has a cinema. And a spa hour is never better than when it is coming down outside.</p>

=== best-time-to-visit-sri-lanka
title: The best time to visit Sri Lanka, and why the answer is two answers

<p>Ask when to visit Sri Lanka and you will be told December to March. That is the answer for one coast. The island is small enough to drive across in a day and large enough to have two monsoons going in opposite directions, which means <strong>there is no bad month, only a wrong coast for that month</strong>.</p>

<h2>The two seasons, in one paragraph</h2>

<p>The <strong>south-west monsoon</strong> runs roughly May to September and brings rain to the west and south coasts and the hill country. The <strong>north-east monsoon</strong> runs roughly October to January and brings it to the east and north. Between them sit two inter-monsoon periods, in October and November and again in March and April, when thunderstorms can arrive anywhere.</p>

<p>So the beaches at Mirissa and Unawatuna are at their best from December to March, and the beaches at Arugam Bay and Trincomalee are at their best from May to September. They are almost exactly out of phase.</p>

<h2>Month by month</h2>

<p><strong>December to March.</strong> The peak. South and west coasts dry and hot, hill country clear, whale watching off Mirissa, and Colombo at its most comfortable. Also the most expensive and the most booked. Christmas and New Year need booking months ahead.</p>

<p><strong>April.</strong> The hottest month, and the one with Sinhala and Tamil New Year in it, which is worth planning around: the country largely stops for several days in the middle of the month, and it is a wonderful time to be here if you want to see it and an inconvenient one if you want to move around.</p>

<p><strong>May to September.</strong> The east coast season. Rain in Colombo in short heavy bursts rather than all day, lower rates everywhere on the west, and the garden at its greenest. Good value and quiet.</p>

<p><strong>October and November.</strong> The wettest weeks in Colombo, and the cheapest. Thunderstorms build in the afternoon and clear by evening. If your trip is a city stay rather than a beach fortnight, this is quietly the best value in the year.</p>

<figure><img src="{{A}}assets/img/bl-court-700.jpg" srcset="{{A}}assets/img/bl-court-700.jpg 700w, {{A}}assets/img/bl-court-1100.jpg 1100w" sizes="(max-width: 760px) 92vw, 46rem" width="700" height="467" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="The planted courtyard at Colombo Court, dense greenery over tables beside the lap pool."><figcaption>The wet months are the ones the garden is grateful for.</figcaption></figure>

<h2>What about Colombo specifically</h2>

<p>Colombo is a city rather than a beach, so the monsoon matters less here than anywhere else on the island. It is between twenty-six and thirty-two degrees every month of the year. What changes is the humidity and the rain, and the rain comes in bursts of under an hour.</p>

<p>The genuinely useful thing to plan around is not the weather but the <strong>Poya days</strong>, the monthly full moon holidays. Alcohol is not served, many places close, and the city is quiet. Ask us which one falls in your week and we will tell you.</p>

<h2>The short version</h2>

<p>Beach on the south or west coast: December to March. Beach on the east coast: May to September. Hill country and tea: January to April. Cultural triangle: any time, though it is very hot in April. Colombo alone: whenever the flight is cheap, and preferably not during a Poya.</p>

=== sri-lankan-breakfast-explained
title: A Sri Lankan breakfast, explained
date: 2026-08-15
topic: Food & Drink
summary: Hoppers, string hoppers, pol sambol, kiribath and a curry before eight in the morning. What each thing is, how to eat it, and why it is worth getting up for.
hero: bl-breakfast-hero

heroAlt: Breakfast laid on a white cloth beside the pool at Amber Poolside, under the trees.

<p>The first Sri Lankan breakfast is a surprise, because it is savoury, it is spiced and it involves curry at seven in the morning. It is also, once you are used to it, the best meal of the day here. This is what is on the table.</p>

<h2>The things made from rice flour</h2>

<p><strong>Hoppers</strong>, or appa. A fermented rice flour and coconut milk batter, swirled around a small round pan so it climbs the sides, giving a bowl with a lacy crisp rim and a soft spongy middle. An <strong>egg hopper</strong> has an egg cracked into that middle. Eat it with your hands, tear from the rim, and use it to scoop.</p>

<p><strong>String hoppers</strong>, or idiyappam. The same flour pressed through a sieve into nests and steamed. They arrive in a stack, they are almost flavourless on their own, and that is the point: they exist to carry curry and sambol.</p>

<p><strong>Kiribath</strong>, milk rice. Rice cooked in coconut milk, pressed flat and cut into diamonds. Slightly sweet, dense, and traditionally the first thing eaten on any auspicious day. Eaten with lunu miris, a fiery onion and chilli paste.</p>

<figure><img src="{{A}}assets/img/bl-pooldine-700.jpg" srcset="{{A}}assets/img/bl-pooldine-700.jpg 700w, {{A}}assets/img/bl-pooldine-1100.jpg 1100w" sizes="(max-width: 760px) 92vw, 46rem" width="700" height="467" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="Tables set for breakfast beside the pool at Colombo Court, greenery all around."><figcaption>Breakfast opens at seven, beside the water.</figcaption></figure>

<h2>The things you put on them</h2>

<p><strong>Pol sambol</strong>, which is scraped coconut, chilli, lime, red onion and Maldive fish. It is on every table and it goes on everything. <strong>Seeni sambol</strong> is the sweet one, caramelised onion with tamarind, and it is gentler. <strong>Lunu miris</strong> is the hot one.</p>

<p>Then the curries. Usually a dhal, a potato or jackfruit curry, and often a fish one. They are mild by Sri Lankan standards at breakfast and the sambol is where the heat lives.</p>

<h2>How to eat it</h2>

<p>With your right hand, if you would like to do it properly, and nobody will mind at all if you use a fork. Tear the hopper, use it as a scoop, mix a little sambol into whatever you are eating rather than eating the sambol on its own. Start with less than you think.</p>

<p>Wash it down with plain tea. Coffee is available everywhere and it is not what this meal is built around.</p>

<h2>Here</h2>

<p>Breakfast at <a href="/eat-drink/#amber">Amber Poolside</a> runs from seven, beside the water under the trees, and the platters go Sri Lankan, Western, Indian and lighter, so nobody has to be brave before they have had coffee. Ask the night before if you want something particular waiting and the kitchen will have it.</p>

<p>Colombo is not a difficult city to move around, but it is one where the right choice changes completely depending on the hour. Here is what each option is actually for.</p>

<h2>Tuk-tuks</h2>

<p>The default, and there is one within thirty seconds of wherever you are standing. Two rules make the whole thing simple.</p>

<p><strong>Use a meter, or use an app.</strong> Metered tuk-tuks have a working meter on the dashboard and are cheap. A driver who will not use the meter is quoting a tourist price, and the polite response is to say no thank you and take the next one, which will be along immediately.</p>

<p><strong>Have small notes.</strong> Nobody has change, ever.</p>

<h2>Ride apps</h2>

<p><strong>PickMe</strong> is the local one and the better one. Uber also operates here. Both do tuk-tuks as well as cars, both show the price before you accept it, and both remove the negotiation entirely. Install PickMe before you arrive, and get a local SIM at the airport so it works.</p>

<p>A car is worth it in rain, with luggage, or after dark. A tuk-tuk is worth it the rest of the time, and is considerably faster in traffic because it fits through gaps a car cannot.</p>

<h2>Buses</h2>

<h2>The verdict</h2>

<p>Worth visiting, not worth rushing, and much better than its reputation. Come expecting a city to be in rather than a city to tick off, and it will be the part of the trip you talk about least in photographs and most at dinner.</p>

=== a-short-guide-to-ceylon-tea
title: A short guide to Ceylon tea
date: 2026-07-11
topic: Food & Drink
summary: Why the country is called Sri Lanka and the tea is still called Ceylon, what the growing altitudes actually taste like, and how to buy some without being sold floor sweepings.
hero: bl-tea-hero
heroW: 1600
heroAlt: A long low sofa and lamps in a lounge at Colombo Court, a place to sit with a pot of tea.
minutes: 5
---
<p>Sri Lanka has been Sri Lanka since 1972 and the tea has been Ceylon the whole time, because the name was worth too much to change. It is one of the world&rsquo;s three great tea origins and almost all of it leaves the island in a blend with somebody else&rsquo;s name on the box.</p>

<h2>Altitude is the thing</h2>

<p>Ceylon tea is graded by how high it grew, and the difference is not marketing. It is the single most useful thing to know.</p>

<p><strong>High-grown</strong>, above 1,200 metres: Nuwara Eliya, Dimbula, Uva. Light in the cup, brisk and astringent, floral. Nuwara Eliya is the lightest and is often called the champagne of Ceylon tea. Uva has a distinct sharp edge that comes from the dry winds in July and August.</p>

<p><strong>Mid-grown</strong>, 600 to 1,200 metres: Kandy. Fuller, coppery, straightforward. The reliable one.</p>

<p><strong>Low-grown</strong>, below 600 metres: Ruhuna and Sabaragamuwa. Dark, malty, strong. This is the tea that takes milk without disappearing, and most of it goes to the Middle East and Russia rather than to Britain.</p>

<h2>The letters on the packet</h2>

<p>BOP, OP, FBOP, Pekoe, Dust. These describe leaf size, not quality. <strong>OP</strong> is a long whole leaf that brews gently. <strong>BOP</strong> is broken orange pekoe, a smaller leaf that brews faster and stronger, and it is what most good Ceylon tea in a pot actually is. <strong>Dust</strong> is what goes in teabags, and it is not an insult, it is simply the fastest.</p>

<p>A <strong>lion logo</strong> on the packet with a sword is the Sri Lanka Tea Board mark. It means the tea is entirely grown, processed and packed in Sri Lanka. Buy tea that has it.</p>

<figure><img src="{{A}}assets/img/bl-ceylontea-700.jpg" srcset="{{A}}assets/img/bl-ceylontea-700.jpg 700w, {{A}}assets/img/bl-ceylontea-1100.jpg 1100w" sizes="(max-width: 760px) 92vw, 46rem" width="700" height="467" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="A Ceylon high tea stand beside the pool, savouries on three tiers with cups of tea alongside."><figcaption>High tea, where most visitors meet the good stuff.</figcaption></figure>

<h2>How it is drunk here</h2>

<p>Strong, and often very sweet. <strong>Plain tea</strong> is black with sugar and no milk. <strong>Kiri te</strong> is with milk. A cup at a roadside stall is poured from a height between two vessels to cool and aerate it, which is worth watching.</p>

<p>The polite thing to know: tea is offered constantly, and accepting is the friendly answer.</p>

<h2>Buying some to take home</h2>

<p>Not at the airport, where it costs three times as much. Buy it in the city from a proper tea merchant, ask for a single-origin from one estate rather than a blend, and ask them to vacuum pack it. A good shop will let you smell the leaf before you commit.</p>

<p>Whole leaf keeps better than dust on a long flight. And if you only buy one thing, make it an Uva from the July or August plucking.</p>

<h2>Trying it here</h2>

<p><a href="/eat-drink/#whats-on">High tea</a> runs from 15:30 to 18:30 daily at LKR 2,800 net, and the price includes a pot. Ask which garden it is from; the answer will be a real one.</p>

<p>A great many people have never had a massage and are slightly embarrassed to ask any of the following. All of it is normal, all of it is asked constantly, and none of it should cost you an hour of quiet wondering.</p>

<h2>What do I wear?</h2>

<p>A robe and slippers are waiting in the room and there is a locker for everything else. Under the towel, wear as much or as little as you are comfortable in. Disposable underwear is provided if you would rather use it. You are covered throughout, with only the part being worked on uncovered, and the therapist will move the towel for you rather than ask you to.</p>

<h2>Do I have to talk?</h2>

<p>No. There is a conversation before the treatment, about what hurts and how much pressure you want, and after that silence is entirely normal and most people prefer it. If you would like to talk, that is fine too. A good therapist will follow your lead within about ninety seconds.</p>

<h2>How much pressure should I ask for?</h2>

<p>More than you think, and say so early. The single most common thing we hear afterwards is that somebody wished they had asked for firmer and did not want to make a fuss. It is not a fuss. Say &ldquo;firmer&rdquo; or &ldquo;lighter&rdquo; at any point and it changes immediately.</p>

<p>Pressure should be intense and never sharp. Discomfort in a tight muscle is the work happening. Pain is not, and you should say so.</p>

=== eco-friendly-hotels-in-colombo
title: What to look for in an eco-friendly hotel in Colombo
date: 2026-07-04
topic: Sustainability
summary: Every hotel in Colombo now has a green claim, and few of them can be checked. Here are the questions that separate a genuine one from a laminated promise, including the ones we would find awkward.
hero: bl-eco-hero
heroW: 1600
heroAlt: The lap pool at Colombo Court between white walls, with a city tower block visible above the roofline.
minutes: 6
---
<p>Every hotel in Colombo now says something about sustainability. Almost none of it is checkable, and a card in the bathroom about towels, useful as it is, is not the same thing as a sustainable hotel. Here is what we would actually ask, including the questions we find uncomfortable.</p>

<h2>Ask what the building used to be</h2>

<p>This is the biggest number in the whole conversation and it is almost never mentioned. The embodied carbon in a concrete frame is enormous, and knocking one down to build something efficient is usually a net loss for decades.</p>

<p>A hotel built inside an existing structure has already done more than one with solar panels on a new build. Colombo Court was two office buildings; the frames stayed, and a court was cut into the middle of them. That is also why no two rooms here are the same shape.</p>

<h2>Ask about water, not just electricity</h2>

<p>Solar panels are visible and get photographed. Water is invisible and matters more in a tropical city.</p>

<p>The questions worth asking: is rainwater harvested? Is grey water recycled into the garden, or does it go to the drain? And is drinking water filtered and bottled on site, or does the hotel buy tens of thousands of plastic bottles a year? That last one is a single number and it is the fastest way to tell whether anyone is really counting.</p>

<figure><img src="{{A}}assets/img/bl-tree-700.jpg" srcset="{{A}}assets/img/bl-tree-700.jpg 700w, {{A}}assets/img/bl-tree-1100.jpg 1100w" sizes="(max-width: 760px) 92vw, 46rem" width="700" height="467" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="A tree sculpted from scrap steel rising through the building towards a glass roof."><figcaption>Scrap metal, welded by Sri Lankan artists. Cheaper than buying art, and better.</figcaption></figure>

<h2>Ask who certified it, and when</h2>

<p>&ldquo;Eco-friendly&rdquo; means nothing on its own. A certification means an outside assessor measured something and will do it again next year.</p>

<p>Ask for the name of the certification and the year it was first awarded. A hotel certified once, five years ago, is telling you about its past. We have been certified <strong>CarbonNeutral since 2011</strong>, which was the first for a city hotel in South Asia, and it is reassessed annually because that is what the certification requires.</p>

<h2>The awkward question</h2>

<p>And here is the one we would ask ourselves. <strong>How much of it is offsetting?</strong></p>

<p>No hotel in Colombo runs at zero. Air conditioning is not optional at thirty degrees, guests arrive on aeroplanes, and a kitchen burns gas. Carbon neutral means the remaining emissions are measured and then offset, and offsetting is not the same as not emitting.</p>

<p>What certification actually buys you is the measuring. The number is counted rather than guessed, it is checked by somebody outside the building, and it goes down most years. Any hotel that will not tell you that part is telling you about its marketing.</p>

<h2>The small tells</h2>

<p>Glass bottles rather than plastic. Reclaimed timber rather than new. Art made from local scrap rather than shipped in. Lighting that is LED everywhere rather than in the corridors. And a garden that is watered with recycled water and looks it in February.</p>

<p>None of these are decisions the guest is asked to make, which is the point. <a href="/blog/what-carbon-neutral-actually-means/">We have written about what ours involves</a>, including the parts that are less flattering.</p>

<p>Colombo does big weddings extremely well, and there are hotels here with ballrooms for eight hundred. This is not about those. It is about the other kind, and about the questions worth asking whichever kind you are planning.</p>

<h2>Ask for real capacities, in three numbers</h2>

<p>A venue that gives you one number is giving you the cocktail number. Ask for three: <strong>seated dinner</strong>, <strong>banquet</strong> and <strong>standing</strong>. They are very different, and the seated one is the one your guests will experience.</p>

<p>Ours, so you can see the shape of it: the garden court seats forty and takes a hundred and twenty for a banquet or a hundred and fifty standing. The rooftop seats sixty. The Scarlet Room takes twenty-five and is a proper meeting room with projection. The library seats eight.</p>

<h2>Ask the monsoon question early</h2>

<p>Any Colombo venue selling you an outdoor evening should volunteer this before you ask. What is the wet-weather plan, how long does it take to enact, and who decides?</p>

<p>An honest answer sounds like a specific room and a specific hour of the day. A dishonest one sounds like reassurance about the season. Rain here arrives in forty-minute bursts, so the answer matters less than most people fear, but the venue should have one.</p>

<figure><img src="{{A}}assets/img/bl-event-700.jpg" srcset="{{A}}assets/img/bl-event-700.jpg 700w, {{A}}assets/img/bl-event-1100.jpg 1100w" sizes="(max-width: 760px) 92vw, 46rem" width="700" height="467" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="Long tables set with white linen and flowers beside the pool at Colombo Court for an event."><figcaption>The court seats forty, or a hundred and twenty at a banquet.</figcaption></figure>

<h2>Ask who you will actually be dealing with</h2>

<p>The person who sells you the venue is often not the person who runs the night. Ask whether it is the same person, and ask to meet them. At a small hotel it will be, which is most of the argument for a small hotel.</p>

<h2>Ask about exclusive use</h2>

<p>This is the question that separates a hotel wedding from a wedding in a hotel. Can you have the whole building? For a thirty-one room property the answer can be yes, which means no strangers in the lift and no other party in the next room. For a two-hundred room hotel it cannot.</p>

<h2>The case for small</h2>

<p>A hundred and twenty people in a garden with a lit pool down the middle of it is a different evening from four hundred in a ballroom, and it costs less per head rather than more. The food comes out of one kitchen rather than a banqueting production line. The photographs have trees in them. And everybody can hear the speeches.</p>

<p>It is also the format that survives Sri Lankan traffic: guests who are staying in the building arrive on time.</p>
